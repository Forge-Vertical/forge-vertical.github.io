The Tesla flying car is real.
It just does not fly the way you think.

On 15 October 2026 a Tesla Roadster drove up a ramp, left the ground on SpaceX-developed thrusters and landed on its wheels. That is a genuine engineering result. It is also a few seconds of controlled hop, not a commute. Here is what was proven, what was not, and what would make it useful.

Jarrit Hosking
Forge Vertical · Cape Town · October 21, 2026
9 min read

Does the Tesla flying Roadster actually fly? Yes, for a few seconds. It uses SpaceX-developed compressed-air thrusters to hop, flip and land, not wings or rotors to stay in the air. It is a working prototype of a car due in 2028, not a vehicle that can carry you over traffic.

// Chapter 01 — What actually happened

A car left the ground. On purpose. And landed on its wheels.

On Thursday 15 October 2026, at SpaceX's test site in McGregor, Texas, Tesla showed a redesigned Roadster to a livestream audience. A white prototype drove up a curved ramp and carried on upside down, held to the surface by suction fans. It left the ramp, rotated in mid-air using bursts of compressed air, and landed on its wheels. In a second routine it fired thrusters to hop over a row of parked Tesla vehicles. Elon Musk's verdict on X: "Turns out the future does have flying cars."

One detail matters more than the stunt itself. Nobody was driving. Reports describe a pre-programmed sequence, with a mannequin in a SpaceX spacesuit in the seat, and Musk said the car ran autonomously for safety reasons.

// Chapter 02 — Credit where it is due

This is real engineering, and it deserves a straight hearing

It is easy to wave this away because of Tesla's record on dates. Do not. The physics on display is genuine. The car carries roughly 600 litres of rechargeable compressed air (about 250 kg, according to event statements relayed by a Tesla fan site) in two pressure vessels. Thrusters on the underside and rear lift it and cushion landings, with smaller side thrusters rotating it in the air. Underbody fans pull it down onto a surface hard enough that Tesla claims cornering above 2 G. Tesla claims 0 to 60 mph in 0.81 seconds. Every one of those figures still needs independent testing, but nothing in the demo contradicts physics.

Some observers asked whether the stunt car was a complete, working vehicle, and one commentator wondered aloud if the event was a prank. A fair reading is that the manoeuvres were real and the prototype was a stunt-grade machine, which is a very different thing from a product.

A hop is not a flight. A stunt is not a product. But a stunt that works is the first step towards both.
// Chapter 03 — Three ways to be in the air

It flies, but not the way an aeroplane flies

Most people hear "flying car" and picture something that stays up. The Roadster does not work like that. It stores a fixed amount of air and spends it.

WINGAeroplane
Fixed wing: lift comes from moving through the air
Wings generate lift continuously, so endurance is limited by fuel or battery, measured in hours. This is how aeroplanes and some roadable-aircraft designs fly.
ROTORHelicopter / eVTOL
Rotors and fans: thrust that can hold a hover
Spinning blades push air down for as long as the battery or fuel lasts. Air taxis and Alef's Model A use fans for vertical take-off. Endurance is minutes to hours.
GASCold-gas thrust
Stored compressed air: a rocket hop
The Roadster releases stored air through nozzles. There is no wing and no rotor, so it only stays up while the air lasts. Think of a very controlled, very short rocket hop. The demonstrated manoeuvres took around three seconds.
Back-of-envelope check (our estimate, not Tesla's numbers). Cold-gas thrusters are usually quoted at a specific impulse of roughly 50 to 80 seconds. Take 250 kg of air and the reported thrust of more than 80 kN. At full power the whole supply lasts about 1.5 to 2.5 seconds. To hover a car of an assumed 1.5 to 2 tonnes, which needs only enough thrust to offset its weight, the best case is roughly 6 to 13 seconds, and real life is worse because not all the air is usable and climbing takes more thrust than hovering. That matches the demo: seconds, not minutes.
// Chapter 04 — Shown and not shown

What the demo proved, and what nobody has published

// Shown in the demo
What the prototype did
  • Drove upside down on a ramp using suction fans
  • Launched, rotated in mid-air and landed on its wheels
  • Hopped over parked vehicles on thruster power
  • Controlled attitude with side thrusters
  • Ran without a driver on a scripted sequence
// Not published
What nobody has told us
  • How long it can stay airborne
  • Maximum height or distance in the air
  • How it performs with a person aboard
  • How long refilling and recompressing takes
  • Occupant safety systems for a flight
  • Independent verification of the headline figures

The history adds context. The second-generation Roadster was unveiled in 2017 with four seats, a claimed 620-mile range and a 1.9 second 0 to 60 time, for a 2020 launch. Today it is a two-seater with a range of "more than 300 miles", with production now planned for 2028.

// Chapter 05 — The straight answer

Is it practical and useful yet?

For getting anywhere, no. Tesla's own website says the Rocket version is not approved for public road use. The FAA told CBS News and Politico the vehicle does not meet its definition of an aircraft, which means it would not need airworthiness certification, and it is unclear which regulator would oversee the flying part. Musk has said, "This is not necessarily a safety-optimized car," and that he is not sure it will be legal in all jurisdictions.

The business case is a halo car, not a commuter. Analyst Dan Ives wrote that it "will not move the volume or revenue needle," and Musk said in 2023 it was "definitely not gonna be a huge contributor to revenue." Reservations start at $50,000 for the Performance version ($5,000 up front and $45,000 within 10 days), the Thrust version has been reported at $250,000, and the single-seat Rocket is invitation-only. Final prices are not announced. The reservation terms say it does not guarantee delivery, and the money is not held in escrow.

// Chapter 06 — Make it useful

What would turn a great stunt into something useful

We like the idea, and that is the point of asking hard questions. A flying vehicle becomes useful when it passes three tests. Today the Roadster passes none of them for transport.

01Time aloft
Long enough to cross something real
A river, a flooded road, a gap in a traffic jam. Seconds of stored air do not get you from A to B. Useful means minutes of controlled movement and a safe landing.
02Category
A legal box to sit in
It must be certifiable as an aircraft, a road vehicle, or both. Right now the FAA says it is not an aircraft and Tesla says it is not for public roads. Without a regulator there is no insurance and no operator licence.
03People aboard
Occupant safety and servicing
Redundancy for a failed thruster, protection in a hard landing, trained service technicians and a refill network. A mannequin does not need any of that. A customer does.

Here are three ways we think the idea could become useful. These are our views, not Tesla's plans.

  • Sell the stunt first. Film, motorsport and show events are places where three seconds of controlled thrust is the whole product, and the demo itself is the proof.
  • Make the hop the product. A controlled hop of a few metres with a soft landing could clear an obstacle gap for rescue or off-road vehicles. That is a narrower and more realistic goal than commuting, but it needs a regulator willing to classify it.
  • Separate the layers. If the goal is transport, the proven routes are fan or wing designs. Alef's Model A, according to New Atlas, received a limited FAA special airworthiness certificate for prototype flight in 2023, drives at about 25 mph and flies roughly 110 miles. Air taxis are still working through certification, and as of late 2025 some analysts did not expect Archer's final type certification until 2028. Keep thrusters for performance, and let real flight be designed as flight.
// Chapter 07 — The builder's read

The questions worth asking about any bold demo

At Forge Vertical we apply the same filter to a Cape Town flyer and a Texas launch event. A demo shows that something can happen once. A product shows it happens every time, for a paying person, inside the rules. Ask five questions: how long, how far, with whom aboard, who certifies it, and who services it? The Roadster has an answer to the first only in seconds and has not published the rest. Ask the same of any vendor pitching you AI, software or infrastructure.

// Verdict Validated: a car can launch, rotate and land on thruster power, and Tesla and SpaceX did it on camera. Not validated: sustained flight, public-road legality, occupant safety, the 2028 date and the headline specs. It is a brilliant stunt and a promising technology demonstration. It is not yet a flying car in the sense most people mean. In South Africa, as anywhere, a vehicle like this would need to satisfy both road and aviation rules, and Tesla itself says legality will vary by jurisdiction.
// Questions answered

Does the Tesla flying Roadster actually fly?

It leaves the ground for a few seconds. In the 15 October 2026 demo the prototype used SpaceX-developed compressed-air thrusters to launch off a ramp, rotate in mid-air and land on its wheels, and to hop over parked cars. That is a controlled hop, not sustained flight like an aeroplane or helicopter, and Tesla has not published how long or how far it can stay airborne.

How does the Tesla flying car work?

It releases stored compressed air through thruster nozzles to push the car up and rotate it in the air, and uses underbody suction fans to hold it to a surface. Tesla's engineering VP Lars Moravy said the car carries about 600 litres of rechargeable compressed air. The thrusters were developed with SpaceX, and Tesla says the air tanks are recharged without fuel.

How long can the Tesla Roadster stay in the air?

Tesla has not published a flight time, altitude or range. The demonstrated manoeuvres lasted a few seconds each, and a back-of-envelope estimate from the reported air supply and thrust suggests the stored air can sustain only seconds of thrust.

When can you buy the Tesla flying Roadster and how much does it cost?

Tesla says production is planned for 2028. Reservations are open: the Performance version needs a $50,000 reservation ($5,000 up front and $45,000 within 10 days), and the Thrust version was reported at $250,000. The single-seat Rocket version with the flight system is invitation-only. Final prices have not been announced, and a reservation does not guarantee delivery.

Is the Tesla flying Roadster legal to fly or drive?

Tesla's website says the Rocket version is not approved for public road use. The FAA told CBS News and Politico that the vehicle does not meet its definition of an aircraft, so it would not need airworthiness certification, and it is unclear which regulator would oversee the flying features. Elon Musk said he is not sure it will be legal in all jurisdictions.

Is the Tesla flying car practical or useful yet?

Not yet. Today it is a halo car and an engineering demonstration: a few seconds of controlled thrust, two seats, no published airborne range and no regulator for the flying part. It becomes useful when it can stay aloft long enough to cross a real obstacle safely, fits a legal vehicle category and protects the people aboard.
