NetworkChuck:
why building cool
stuff is enough of a reason.

5.3 million subscribers. 400 million views. A beard, a coffee, and a genuine belief that technology is one of the most exciting things a human being can spend time on. This is a personal note on why NetworkChuck matters — and why more of us should build things just because they are interesting.

Jarrit Hosking
Forge Vertical · Cape Town · August 19, 2026
8 min read
// Chapter 01 — The person who made IT feel like a superpower

There is a specific feeling that Chuck Keith — NetworkChuck — produces in his videos. It is not exactly excitement, though excitement is part of it. It is something closer to permission. Permission to find this stuff genuinely interesting. Permission to stay up until midnight setting up a home server not because it is productive but because it is cool. Permission to learn something new for no commercial reason whatsoever, just because the technology is fascinating and building things is one of the genuinely pleasurable things a human being can do.

NetworkChuck — the online persona of Chuck Keith — is an American YouTube educator, IT content creator, and entrepreneur based in the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex. His YouTube channel launched on April 27, 2014, and has grown to more than 5.3 million subscribers. As of 2026 it has published over 600 videos with 410 million total views, with a 4.03% engagement rate — placing it in the top 20% of AI and coding educators.

The numbers are impressive. They are not the point. The point is what he does with the camera on.

"NetworkChuck is, by far, the single best tech teacher on YouTube. His greatest strength is knowing exactly how to frame his lessons in simple, human terms." — NetworkChuck Academy student review

What he actually does

// Chapter 02 — The format that works

The format is deceptively simple. Chuck picks a technology — a networking concept, a Linux command, an AI tool, a homelab build — and he does it on camera. Not explains it. Does it. He makes mistakes. He figures it out in real time. He drinks his coffee and gets genuinely enthusiastic about things that most people would find intimidating or dry.

NetworkChuck makes technical topics approachable with high-energy, hands-on tutorials. His AI content focuses on practical application: how to actually use, deploy, and build with AI tools rather than just understanding the theory. That distinction matters. Most technical education teaches you what something is. Chuck teaches you how to do it, while you watch him do it, in a way that makes you want to open your own terminal immediately after.

The series progressions are specific. "You Suck at Subnetting" addresses IP addressing challenges in nine lessons using practical examples like subnetting a home network or a coffee shop. Individual episodes have garnered millions of views. He named a series "You Suck at Subnetting." That is not a person trying to seem authoritative. That is a person who understands exactly who he is talking to and meets them where they are.

The coffee is also part of it. His X bio reads: "Believer. Beard. Coffee. Tech. Youtube." In that order. The coffee is not incidental — it is a signal that this is a real person doing something he genuinely loves, not a polished corporate production. You cannot fake that energy over 600 videos and 400 million views.

Why this matters beyond entertainment

// Chapter 03 — The thing he is actually teaching

The surface content is networking, Linux, cybersecurity, AI tools, homelabs, cloud infrastructure. But the deeper curriculum — the thing that accumulates across hundreds of hours of watching — is a disposition toward technology itself.

The disposition is this: technology is something you can understand. It is not magic. It is not exclusively for people with computer science degrees or corporate IT jobs. It is a set of tools that any person with curiosity and access to a terminal can pick up, play with, break, fix, and eventually master. The barrier is not intelligence. It is permission — the belief that this is something you are allowed to do.

Chuck gives that permission freely, repeatedly, enthusiastically, without condescension. He makes the person watching feel like a peer who is slightly behind, not like a civilian watching an expert perform. That is a genuinely rare skill in education, and it is the reason his channel has the engagement rate it does.

For someone like me — self-taught, no CS degree, learning everything through tutorials, documentation, and trial and error — that disposition is not just useful. It is the precondition for everything else. You cannot learn something you believe is beyond you. Chuck is very good at making you believe it is not beyond you.

The Chuck principle: build something, then build something else.

The AI chapter

// Chapter 04 — Where he is going

Chuck has always covered new technology as it arrived. What is interesting about the current AI moment is how naturally it fits his format. AI tools are exactly the kind of thing that benefits from someone doing it on camera rather than explaining it in slides. You cannot fully understand what Ollama does or how Claude Code works until you watch someone use it on a real project, in a real terminal, making real decisions.

His recent content covers Claude Code, Gemini CLI, AI agents, n8n workflow automation, and AI terminal tools — with cybersecurity and homelab content continuing alongside. The breadth is intentional. He is not pivoting to AI. He is treating AI the same way he treated networking and Linux — as a technology worth understanding hands-on, by building things with it, by making mistakes with it on camera.

He noted on X: "I love using AI but the moments where I'm not using it — that deep work when I'm not reaching for the terminal — I'm clear, focused, joyful, productive. We still need those deep 'no AI' work sessions." That is a more honest and useful observation about AI than most of what gets published by people whose entire brand is AI enthusiasm. He uses it. He loves it. He also knows when not to use it. That is what actual technical maturity looks like.

Why I find him inspiring

// Chapter 05 — Personal

I built Forge Vertical, TripSpace Global, Aurora Repair, and a collection of other things without a computer science degree, without a team, without external funding. The foundation of that was the belief — absorbed from years of watching people like Chuck — that technology is learnable, that building things is possible, and that the gap between where you are and where you want to be is bridged by starting, not by waiting until you are ready.

Chuck did not teach me any specific thing I can point to. He taught me the disposition. The willingness to open the terminal without knowing exactly what I was doing. The comfort with breaking things and fixing them. The genuine enthusiasm for what technology can do when you stop being intimidated by it and start being curious about it.

That is a harder thing to teach than subnetting or Docker or AI agents. And he does it every week, with a coffee, in front of 5.3 million people.
