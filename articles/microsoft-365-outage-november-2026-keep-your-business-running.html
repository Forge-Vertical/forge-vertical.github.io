The Microsoft 365 outage:
how to keep your business running when big tech goes down.

What happened, what Microsoft has said, why it stopped so many businesses, and a plain plan for the next time a vendor goes down.

Jarrit Hosking
Forge Vertical · Cape Town · November 15, 2026
7 min read

What happened? On Thursday 12 November 2026, Microsoft 365 failed for many users around the world. Email in Exchange Online stopped working for many people, and Teams, SharePoint, Copilot and Defender were also listed as affected. Microsoft said it was a technical fault linked to a network capacity upgrade, not a cyberattack. Recovery was gradual and ran into Friday and Saturday. For a lot of businesses the real problem was not the outage itself. It was how much of the business sat behind that one vendor.

01 / What happened

A day that began with Teams and ended with email

The first problem was small. According to BleepingComputer, a separate Teams incident hit users in North and South America that morning, lasting about 68 minutes. Microsoft blamed resource contention and said it was resolved.

The main outage started in the afternoon. Microsoft's advisory puts the start at about 1:26 pm US Eastern time on 12 November. Users in the Americas could not reach their Exchange Online mailboxes. Microsoft later confirmed that users in any region could be affected, and the service listing grew to include Teams, SharePoint Online, Microsoft 365 Copilot and Microsoft Defender XDR.

The scale was large. Outage trackers recorded tens of thousands of reports in the US alone, and TechRadar's live coverage counted a peak of more than 131,000 Outlook reports and spread across at least 74 countries. Those are tracker figures, not Microsoft's, and should be read as a guide to scale rather than an exact count. The UK government, local councils and police forces warned the public of disruption to email and online forms.

02 / The cause

What Microsoft has said so far

Microsoft's public explanation changed as the day went on. First it blamed elevated load. Then it said a number of servers had become unexpectedly unavailable, which strained the rest of the infrastructure. Later it said a core process on the affected servers was overwhelmed. On 13 November Microsoft said the incident was “introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade,” and that it was “a technical issue not related to cyber or AI,” as reported by Forbes.

Microsoft paused deployments, moved traffic away from affected systems, restarted servers and extended the email timeout window to 48 hours so delayed messages would not be lost. It apologised, and said it will publish a full post-incident report. At the time of writing, the full post-incident report had not been published, so anything beyond the above is speculation.

Was Azure down too? Reports of Azure problems did appear on outage trackers. But Microsoft's own list centred on Microsoft 365 services, and it has not said Azure as a whole failed. This is more accurately a Microsoft 365 outage than a full Azure outage.
03 / Not the first time

Every big provider has a day like this

It would be easy to read this as a Microsoft problem. It is not only that. On 29 October 2025, an inadvertent configuration change to Azure Front Door caused a global disruption of roughly eight hours, which ThousandEyes analysed in detail. It touched the Azure Portal, Microsoft Entra ID and other services, and it also slowed engineers down, because the portal they would use to fix things was itself affected.

The same applies to every large provider. On 12 June 2025 Google Cloud had a global incident: a policy change with blank fields was replicated worldwide within seconds and crashed a core service in a loop, affecting around 70 Google Cloud products. Google's incident report is a good read, and it is open about the cause.

South Africans have seen a different version of the same story. On 14 March 2024, Microsoft 365 and Teams became unreachable across EMEA around lunchtime, and Bizcommunity reported the cause as multiple undersea cable failures. The Citizen reported that most of the complaints came from South Africa and the UK, with Downdetector logging 6,489 reports by 13:32. The fault was reportedly in the network path rather than in Microsoft's software, and the effect on businesses was the same.

04 / Why it hurts

The problem is concentration, not Microsoft

When one vendor carries your email, chat, calendar, documents, sign-in and sometimes your job tracking too, one outage is not an inconvenience. It is the whole business stopping at once. Staff cannot reach customers. Customers cannot reach staff. The quote that was meant to go out at two o'clock does not go out.

That is what many offices felt on 12 November. People could not do their work, and it did not matter how well they did their jobs. The tools they depended on were not there.

  • Email is a messenger, not a record. If your only copy of a customer's request lives in an inbox, an outage hides it.
  • Sign-in is a single point. If one account system unlocks everything, one failure locks everything.
  • Chat becomes the process. When approvals and job updates live in a chat thread, there is no way to carry on without the chat.
05 / What to do

A plain plan for the next outage

You do not need a large budget. You need a short list of decisions made before the day, not on it.

  1. Write down what depends on what. One page: email, chat, files, sign-in, your job or order system, payments, and which vendor each one sits with.
  2. Keep a second way to reach people. A phone tree, a WhatsApp group for staff, and a way to reach key customers that does not run through your email provider.
  3. Separate the system that runs the work from the system that carries the conversation. Jobs, quotes, invoices and customer records should not disappear when email does.
  4. Export your important data regularly. If you can get your customer list and open jobs out in minutes, you can keep going.
  5. Agree who does what. Who tells staff, who calls the big customers, who watches the status page.
  6. Test it once. Pretend email is down for a morning and see what breaks.

Point three is the one many small businesses overlook, and it is where a purpose-built system earns its keep. If your operations run in their own system, with their own sign-in and their own record of every change, a mail outage becomes a communications problem instead of a business-stopping one.

06 / What a resilient system looks like

Built to fail gracefully, not to never fail

Honest resilience is not a claim that nothing will go wrong. It is a set of choices that limit how far a failure spreads.

ChoiceWhen one vendor goes down
Operations separate from email and chatJobs, quotes and records stay available
Its own sign-in and rolesStaff can still log in, and still see only their part
Full audit logYou can see what changed, and who did it, after any incident
Data you can exportYou can move or keep running if you must
Owned code and hostingYou are not stuck waiting on a vendor's roadmap

None of this removes dependence on a cloud. A custom system on Google Cloud still depends on Google Cloud, and a system on Azure still depends on Azure. The point is to avoid stacking every dependency on the same one, and to choose, on purpose, which parts of the business must keep running.

07 / The takeaway

Keep Microsoft. Stop betting the whole business on it.

Microsoft 365 is still a good product, and for most businesses email, calendar and documents are best rented. The outage did not prove otherwise. It showed what happens when everything is one failure away from stopping. A short plan, a second channel, and an operations system you control are cheap protection against a very expensive day.

If you run a franchise, several branches or a business that cannot afford a quiet day, it is worth thinking about now rather than the next time.

// Questions answered

What caused the November 2026 Microsoft 365 outage?

Microsoft said it was a technical issue, not a cyberattack and not related to AI. It said the problem was introduced during an earlier network capacity upgrade, and that a number of servers became unexpectedly unavailable, which put the remaining servers under heavy load. Microsoft has said it will publish a full post-incident report, so more detail may follow.

How long did the Microsoft 365 outage last?

It began on Thursday 12 November 2026, at around 1:26 pm US Eastern time according to Microsoft's advisory, and recovery was gradual. By midday on Friday 13 November email queues were approaching normal levels, and reporting on Saturday said the outage had been resolved after more than a day, with Microsoft still restoring some customers and regions.

Was Azure down as well?

Reports of Azure problems appeared on outage trackers and in some coverage, but Microsoft's own advisory centred on Microsoft 365 services: Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint Online, Microsoft 365 Copilot and Microsoft Defender XDR. Microsoft has not said that Azure as a whole failed, so it is more accurate to call this a Microsoft 365 outage than an Azure outage.

Was the outage a cyberattack?

Microsoft said it found no evidence of malicious or cyber activity. Its public explanation was a technical fault linked to a network capacity upgrade and overloaded infrastructure.

Is this the first time Microsoft has had a major outage?

No. On 29 October 2025 an inadvertent configuration change to Azure Front Door caused a global disruption of about eight hours that affected the Azure Portal, Microsoft Entra ID and other services. On the same day as the November 2026 outage, a separate Teams incident also lasted about an hour. Every large cloud provider has had major incidents, including Google Cloud on 12 June 2025.

Should my business stop using Microsoft 365?

Not necessarily. For most businesses email, calendars and documents are still best bought as a service. The lesson is not to leave, but to avoid putting everything behind one vendor. Keep the systems that run your daily operations separate from your email and chat, so one outage does not stop the work.

How can a business keep working when Microsoft or another vendor is down?

Know what depends on what, keep a second way to reach staff and customers (phone or WhatsApp), keep your operations system independent of your email and chat, export your important data regularly, and agree in advance who does what during an outage. A written one-page plan is enough to start.

Does a custom business system avoid outages?

No system is immune, including one built on Google Cloud or Microsoft Azure. What a custom system can do is fail more gracefully: it can run separately from your email and chat, show each person only what they need, keep a full record of changes, and let you export your data at any time.
