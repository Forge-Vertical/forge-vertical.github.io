Respect goes both ways:
five customer habits that start a business relationship badly.

Most people do not mean to be disrespectful in business. Here is where it creeps in, why it costs you, and what to say instead.

Jarrit Hosking
Forge Vertical · Cape Town · November 16, 2026
6 min read

What is this about? Many customers disrespect the businesses they buy from without ever meaning to. They name the price the work should cost, compare a small business to a giant, tell the owner how to run things, get sarcastic about a quote, or carry a bad experience from one supplier into the next. None of it is meant as an attack, and all of it starts the relationship on a bad footing. This article is about why, and what works better. Respect should go both ways.

01 / The big picture

Everyone is a customer to someone

The panelbeater is a customer of the paint supplier. The restaurant owner is a customer of the landlord, the butcher and the electricity provider. The person asking for a discount this morning is probably being asked for one this afternoon by their own client.

Almost everyone sits on both sides of the counter. That is why this matters. The way you treat the business in front of you is, in a very practical sense, the way you would like your own customers to treat you.

I have noticed how often people are disrespectful in business without realising it. It is rarely malice. It is habit, and it usually comes from not knowing what sits behind a price. Here are five of the most common habits, and what to do instead.

02 / Habit one

Telling a business what its price should be

A customer sees a quote. What they do not see is everything underneath it.

Take an established panelbeating business. A repair done properly involves preparation, paint, a booth, trained people, insurance, premises, a warranty and someone you can come back to if it goes wrong. Asking that business for a backyard price is not a negotiation. It is asking them to remove everything that makes it an established business. A backyard repair and a workshop repair can look similar in a photo. They are not the same job.

The same is true of food. A plate of food is not priced from what is on the plate. The ingredients are one line. Then there are the staff, the rent, the electricity, the equipment, the waste, the licences, the hours of preparation before the doors open and the meals that never get sold. Telling a restaurant what its food is worth, with no idea of what it takes to put it in front of you, tells the owner you have not thought about it.

You do not need to be an expert in someone's field to buy from them. You only need to be curious before you are critical. Ask what is included. Ask what you are paying for. Then decide whether it suits you.

03 / Habit two

Comparing a small business to a medium or big one

“Why can the big shop do it for less?” is a fair question with a simple answer: they are not the same business.

A large company buys in bulk, spreads its costs across a very large number of customers and runs on process. A small business carries its own costs on a much smaller base, and gives you something a big one often cannot: the owner's attention, flexibility, and a person who is accountable for the result. Neither is better. They are different offers, at different prices, for different needs.

The fair comparison is like for like. Put the same scope in front of each, then compare materials, warranty, turnaround and who actually does the work. A cheaper price that leaves half of that out has not beaten anyone. It has quoted something smaller.

04 / Habit three

Telling a business how to run itself

“Why don't you just...” is one of the most common phrases a business owner hears, and one of the least useful.

A customer sees the front of the business. They do not see the supplier terms, the wage bill, the mistakes that taught the owner a lesson, or the reasons a rule exists. Advice from someone who has never carried those things is usually advice about a business that does not exist.

There is a useful line here. Feedback is what happened to you: “I waited forty minutes and nobody told me why.” That helps a business. Instruction is what they should do about it: “You need to hire more people.” That does not. Share the first, be careful with the second, and offer advice when someone asks for it.

05 / Habit four

Calling a business overpriced, or getting sarcastic

Sarcasm is a way of refusing to have a conversation. “Wow, that is expensive”, “Must be nice” and “For that price it should cook itself” do not lower the price. They only tell the business that you are not worth the time.

If the price does not suit you, you do not have to explain it with an insult. You can say it plainly:

  • “That is above my budget. What can I get for this amount?” This opens a smaller scope, a phased approach or a simpler option.
  • “Thank you, I am going to pass.” This leaves the door open for next time.

In my experience, the customers who are polite about price are the ones businesses go further for. People help people they like. That is not a trick. It is how working relationships have always worked.

06 / Habit five

Bringing the last supplier's mistakes to the new one

A bad experience is real, and it makes sense to be careful afterwards. But the new business has not done anything yet. If you arrive suspicious, quoting everything the last one got wrong, you are asking a stranger to pay for someone else's failure.

It also works against you. A defensive customer is harder to serve well, because the business spends its effort managing the mood instead of doing the work.

There is a better way to use what you learned. Tell the new business what went wrong as information, early, and ask for the thing that would have prevented it. “Last time the price changed halfway through, so I would like the scope and cost agreed in writing” is a fair, professional request. Any good business will say yes to it, and the relationship starts on firm ground instead of on suspicion.

08 / The other half

Respect has to come from the business too

None of this is a one-way street, and it would be unfair to write it as one. A customer who has never bought what you sell cannot know what it costs, and teaching them is part of the job.

  • Explain the price in plain language. Do not hide behind jargon.
  • Put quotes in writing, with what is included and what is not.
  • Do not talk down to people who do not know your trade. Not knowing is not a character flaw.
  • Answer messages and keep promises. Most bad reviews begin with silence.
  • Own your mistakes and fix them without a fight.

A business that treats its customers well earns the right to expect the same back. And a customer who treats a business well usually finds out how good that business can be.

09 / The takeaway

Be curious before you are critical

Before you comment on someone's price, size or way of working, ask yourself one question: how would I feel if my customer said this to me? If the answer is “not great”, say something else. Ask a question. State a budget. Say thank you.

Businesses are built by people, and people remember how they were treated. That counts on both sides of the counter.

// Questions answered

Is it rude to ask a business for a discount?

No. Asking is fine, and many businesses have room to adjust scope, timing or payment terms. What lands badly is demanding a number with no understanding of what the price covers, or being sarcastic about it. A better way is to say what your budget is and ask what you can get for it.

Why is a small business more expensive than a big chain?

They are not selling the same thing under the same conditions. A large company buys in bulk and spreads its costs across a very large number of customers. A small business carries its own tools, premises, insurance and time, and usually offers more direct attention and flexibility. Compare scope, materials, warranty and turnaround rather than the headline price.

How do I compare quotes fairly?

Ask each business to quote the same scope in writing, then compare what is included: materials, labour, warranty, timeline, who does the work and what happens if something goes wrong. A lower price that leaves things out is not a like-for-like comparison.

What if a previous supplier let me down?

Say so, as information, early on. Something like "last time the price changed halfway through, so I would like the scope and price agreed in writing" is a fair request that helps the new business serve you well. Treating the new business as guilty before it has done anything makes the relationship harder for both of you.

How should a business respond when a customer says it is overpriced?

Calmly, and without taking the bait. Explain what is included, offer a smaller scope or phased option if that is possible, and be comfortable saying no to a price that does not work. Respect applies to the business too: explain things in plain language and do not talk down to people who do not know your trade.

Why does respect matter in business if the customer is always right?

Because a good working relationship needs both sides. Customers deserve honest quotes, clear communication and fair treatment. Businesses deserve to be treated as experts in their own field. Nearly everyone is a customer to someone else, so the standard you ask for is the standard you should give.
