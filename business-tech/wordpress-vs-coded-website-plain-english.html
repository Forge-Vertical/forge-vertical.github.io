WordPress or a coded site?
The difference, in plain English.

WordPress has run a huge part of the web since 2003. Here is what it is good at, where it struggles, how it differs from a site written for your business, and when it is worth switching.

Jarrit Hosking
Forge Vertical · Cape Town · October 29, 2026
10 min read

What is the difference between a WordPress site and a coded website? A WordPress site is built from ready-made parts that a database assembles every time someone visits. A coded website is written for your business and contains only what your pages need. WordPress is not bad, and it suits plenty of people. But if your site is slow, hard to find on Google or constantly needing fixes, a coded site is often faster, safer and cheaper to look after.

A fair warning. Forge Vertical builds coded websites, so we have an interest in this question. That is why this article also tells you when to stay on WordPress, and why every figure below comes with its source at the bottom.
// Chapter 01 — The simple picture

A kit house versus a house built for you

Think of two ways to get a house. One is a kit: walls, windows and roofs made in a factory so they fit many different buyers. The other is built on your plot, to your plan, with nothing you did not ask for.

WordPress is the kit. It comes with thousands of ready-made parts, called themes and plugins. A plugin might add a contact form, a gallery or a slider. You pick what you want and click to install it. That is why it is quick and cheap to start.

A coded website is the house built for you. A developer writes exactly what your pages need. If you want a contact form, the form is written for your business. There are no spare rooms and no parts for features you never use.

Neither is wrong. They are different ways to build, with different strengths and different long-term costs.

// Chapter 02 — Where WordPress came from

It arrived in 2003, and the world has moved on

WordPress was released in May 2003, back when a website was mostly text and a few pictures, and blogging was the new thing. It was built to make publishing easy for people who could not write code, and it did that very well. Today it runs about 42.6% of all websites, according to the W3Techs figures reported by Barn2, which is more than any other website builder.

Software that has been around for more than twenty years has been patched, extended and added to many times. Think about your phone. Apps are updated all the time because phones change, people's habits change and attackers find new weak spots. A website is no different. It has to keep up with how people browse today: on phones, on slow mobile data, and increasingly through AI assistants that read websites to answer questions.

WordPress has kept up, and it is still being updated. But it carries all those years of history, and every extra plugin you add carries its own.

// Chapter 03 — Cheap to expensive

"WordPress site" can mean very different things

This is the part many business owners miss. Two WordPress sites can be completely different products.

At the cheap end is a single page that looks like a flyer or leaflet: a logo, a few paragraphs, a phone number. At the expensive end is a carefully built site with proper search settings, speed work and security. In between there is everything else.

What you get depends largely on the experience of the person who built it. A WordPress site is only as good as its builder, because the system lets someone assemble a page-looking website without ever setting up the things you cannot see.

And we have seen those missing things. In real business websites we have come across:

  • No search settings at all. Pages with no proper title or description, so Google shows something random or nothing useful.
  • No schema. Schema is a set of labels, hidden behind the page, that tell Google and AI assistants what your business is, where it is and what it sells. Without it they have to guess.
  • A robots file that did not work. This is a small note at the front door of your site that tells search engines what they may read. On some sites it was missing or broken, so the site was effectively not set up for search engines at all.

You can check the last one yourself in ten seconds. Type your web address into a browser and add /robots.txt on the end, like yourbusiness.co.za/robots.txt. You should see a short page of plain text. If you see an error page, ask whoever built your site why.

// Chapter 04 — The robots file

A lazy robots file versus a well designed one

The robots file is the first thing a search engine or AI assistant reads when it visits your site. Think of it as a sign on the front door telling visitors which rooms they may enter. Most WordPress sites ship with a lazy one, or one that was set up once and never looked at again.

Two lazy examples we see all the time:

User-agent: *
Disallow: /cgi-bin
User-agent: *
Disallow:

Neither of these will break your site. The first only shuts one back-room folder that most sites do not even use. The second says "everyone is welcome", which is fine as far as it goes. The problem is what they leave out. They do not point to your sitemap, which is the list of every page you want found. They say nothing about the AI assistants that now answer questions about local businesses. And they give you no clear record of what you decided, so if a plugin or a host quietly adds a block later, nobody notices.

What a well designed one looks like:

User-agent: *
Allow: /

User-agent: GPTBot
Allow: /

User-agent: ChatGPT-User
Allow: /

User-agent: OAI-SearchBot
Allow: /

User-agent: Google-Extended
Allow: /

User-agent: CCBot
Allow: /

User-agent: anthropic-ai
Allow: /

User-agent: ClaudeBot
Allow: /

User-agent: Claude-Web
Allow: /

User-agent: PerplexityBot
Allow: /

User-agent: Applebot-Extended
Allow: /

User-agent: Bytespider
Allow: /

Sitemap: https://www.your-business.co.za/sitemap.xml

Each block welcomes one named visitor. The first covers everybody. The rest name the crawlers behind the main AI assistants and search tools, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Apple and Google's AI products, plus Common Crawl, which many AI systems learn from. The last line points them to your sitemap. Swap www.your-business.co.za for your own address, and make sure that sitemap really exists.

Does it work? It is one part of a bigger picture. On our own sites, Cloudflare's crawler report shows the major search and AI crawlers visiting every week. But a robots file only opens the door. Being found and mentioned also depends on a sitemap, schema, good content and other places that mention you. We use all of them together.

One more honest note: it is your choice who to welcome. If you do not want a particular crawler on your site, change its Allow: / to Disallow: /. Bytespider, from TikTok's parent company ByteDance, is the one owners most often decide to block. And if your site sits behind Cloudflare, check its AI crawler settings as well, because they can block crawlers even when your robots file welcomes them.

// Chapter 05 — Speed

Why WordPress can feel heavy

When someone visits a WordPress site, the site does a lot of work before showing anything. It asks its database for your page, collects the theme, runs each plugin, and puts it all together. Every plugin adds a little more to carry, and many sites run a dozen or more.

A coded site skips most of that. The pages are built ahead of time, so a visitor simply gets the finished page. There is no database to wait for and no plugins to load.

You may be told that animations, effects and fancy designs need WordPress. They do not. Anything you can see on a WordPress site, whether an animation, a hover effect, a sliding gallery or a particular look and feel, can be built on a coded website without the extra weight. In most cases the difference in speed is something you notice the first time you tap a page.

We will not promise a number in an article, because every site is different. What we do is run Google's free PageSpeed test on your current site before we start and again when we finish, and hand you both results.

// Chapter 06 — Safety

Plugins are where most of the danger sits

Every plugin is code written by someone else, which you then trust with your business. When one has a weak spot, the sites using it are exposed until it is fixed and every owner remembers to update.

Patchstack, a company that tracks this, counted 11,334 new weak spots in the WordPress world during 2025, an increase of 42% on the year before. About 91% were in plugins, and only six were in WordPress itself. Nearly half (46%) had no fix available from the developer at the time they were made public. Patchstack also tested the protections that many hosting companies provide and found they blocked only a small share of attacks on known weak spots.

That does not mean your WordPress site will be hacked. It means a WordPress site needs someone keeping watch, updating, and removing plugins you no longer use. A coded site has far fewer moving parts to watch, because it has no plugins and nothing that needs a login to run.

// Chapter 08 — The honest part

WordPress is not bad, and big companies use it too

Some of the biggest names in publishing and business run on WordPress. But they do not run it the way a small business does. They pay for managed hosting built for large WordPress sites, engineers who test every update before it goes live, security monitoring, speed work and specialists who look after it full time. That extra infrastructure is what makes it work at that level.

A small business on a standard hosting plan, with an off-the-shelf theme and a pile of plugins, is in a very different position. Same name, wildly different setup.

Stay on WordPress if:

  • You or your staff write new posts or change content every week and want to do it yourselves.
  • Your site is already fast, well set up and updated by someone you trust.
  • You run a shop or membership site that is already working well on it.

Think about a coded site if:

  • Your site is slow, especially on a phone.
  • You rarely change the content, but you keep paying to maintain it.
  • Google barely shows you, or AI assistants never mention your business.
  • You have been told your site needs yet another plugin or another fix.
  • You want it to look like your business rather than like a template.
// Chapter 09 — Cost of switching

Switching may cost less than you think

If you built your business on WordPress years ago, switching can feel like an expense you do not need. Fair enough. But the comparison is not a new site against nothing. It is a new site against what you are already paying: the hosting, the plugin renewals, the updates, the emergency fixes and the customers who left because the page took too long to load.

Add those up over a few years before you decide. For many small sites, a coded site pays for itself.

// Questions answered

What is the difference between WordPress and a coded website?

WordPress builds each page from ready-made themes and plugins, using a database every time someone visits. A coded website is written specifically for your business and published as finished pages, so it contains only what it needs. WordPress is easier for you to edit yourself. A coded site is usually faster and has fewer things to maintain.

Is WordPress bad for business websites?

No. WordPress runs about 42.6% of all websites and suits many businesses, especially those that update content often and want to do it themselves. Problems usually come from poor setup, too many plugins, missing search settings or no one keeping it updated.

Why is my WordPress site so slow?

Usually because it is carrying too much: a heavy theme, many plugins, large pictures and a database that has to put each page together on every visit. Reducing plugins and images can help. A coded site avoids most of that work because the pages are ready before the visitor arrives.

Is a coded website safer than WordPress?

Generally it has far fewer weak spots, because it has no plugins and nothing public to log in to. Patchstack counted 11,334 new weak spots in the WordPress world in 2025, 91% of them in plugins. A WordPress site can be kept safe, but it needs regular attention.

Can you rebuild my WordPress site without losing my Google ranking?

That is the aim. With your permission we copy your content and web addresses, set old addresses to point to the new ones, and set up titles, descriptions and schema properly. No one can promise a ranking, but we aim to keep what you already have and improve on it.

Will I still be able to change things on a coded website?

Not by logging in and typing, the way you do on WordPress. Changes go through your developer. At Forge Vertical, our Website Retainer covers regular updates each month, so you ask and we change it.

How do I check whether my website has a robots file?

Type your web address in a browser and add /robots.txt at the end. You should see a short page of plain text. If you see an error page, the file is missing or not working.

What should a robots.txt file contain?

At minimum, a rule welcoming all crawlers, a pointer to your sitemap, and ideally named permissions for the main AI crawlers such as GPTBot, ChatGPT-User, OAI-SearchBot, Google-Extended, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot and Applebot-Extended. It opens the door to crawlers, and on our own sites they visit every week. Whether an AI assistant then names your business depends on your sitemap, schema, content and the rest of your online presence.
